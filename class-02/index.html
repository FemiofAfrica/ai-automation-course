02What "Context" Actually Means

People use "context" to mean five different things, then wonder why the advice they read does not work. Here are the five, kept strictly separate. You will use these words all day.

1 of 5

Instructions

What you want done, and the standing rules for how to do it. This includes your typed request and any permanent settings such as project instructions or custom instructions.

"Draft a LinkedIn post about the new integration." Plus the standing rule: "Always check the do-not-say list first."

2 of 5

Context

The reference material the model can read while answering this specific request: your voice guide, your persona notes, your product facts. Facts about your world, not commands.

Your Brand Voice Guide note. It does not tell the model what to do; it tells the model what is true about you.

3 of 5

Memory

What carries over between sessions without you re-pasting it. Chat history, saved preferences, notes the tool keeps about you. It accumulates by use, not by curation.

"Last Tuesday we agreed the healthcare angle was too risky." Memory is a transcript of what happened, not a curated fact sheet.

4 of 5

Tools

The abilities that let the model fetch or change things: web search, a file reader, your CRM. Tools are how new context arrives mid-task rather than being supplied up front.

A web search tool pulls today's competitor pricing into the answer. Week 3 is entirely about this layer.

5 of 5

Retrieval

The selection step: choosing which slice of your knowledge base gets loaded for this request. Search, filtering, or the model reading a specific file by name.

You have 80 notes. The request is about email. Retrieval pulls the voice guide, the email tone rules, and one persona. Not all 80.

The relationship

How they fit together

Instructions say what to do. Context says what is true. Memory says what happened before. Tools fetch what is missing. Retrieval decides what to load.

Today you build context and retrieval. Week 3 adds tools. Week 4 adds memory. Instructions you already know.

The distinction that saves you money and embarrassment

Memory is not a knowledge base. Memory is a by-product of chatting: it grows on its own, it keeps whatever was said including things you later reversed, and you do not fully control it. A knowledge base is deliberately curated: you decide what goes in, you correct it, you delete what is wrong. When a stale fact keeps resurfacing, the fix is almost always to correct the curated note, not to argue with memory.

03The Context Window, and Why More Is Not Better

Every model has a limit on how much it can hold in view at once. That limit is the context window, measured in tokens (roughly three quarters of a word each, as we covered in Class 1). Two things matter about it, and the second one surprises people.

Fact 1: it is finite

Everything competes for the same space: your instructions, your pasted documents, the tool results, the conversation so far, and the answer being written. Fill it with noise and there is less room for what matters.

Practical effect: paste a 90-page PDF and ask a narrow question, and you have spent your budget on 89 irrelevant pages.

Fact 2: quality degrades before the limit

Anthropic's engineering team documents this directly. They describe an attention budget and cite research on context rot: as the number of tokens goes up, the model's ability to accurately recall information from that context goes down. It affects every model, gently in some, sharply in others.

So "just paste everything, the window is big" is a real mistake, not a stylistic preference. It is a documented failure mode.

The rule the whole session hangs on

Anthropic states the goal as finding "the smallest possible set of high-signal tokens that maximize the likelihood of some desired outcome." Translated for your job: give the model the tightest possible set of things that are actually true and actually relevant. Not the biggest pile. The tightest useful set.

Context quality, in the four checks you can actually run

Relevant Your whole vault dumped in for a single tweet Voice guide plus the one persona the tweet targets

"Professional yet friendly" is the single most common piece of dead weight in a brand document. It is not context, because it excludes nothing. Real context excludes things.

On the phrase "prompt engineering is dead"

It is not dead, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling something. Clear instructions still matter, and Anthropic still publishes prompt engineering guidance. What has changed is where the leverage sits. Anthropic frames context engineering as "the natural progression of prompt engineering": with capable models, the bigger win now comes from curating what the model knows rather than re-wording what you ask. Both matter. Context is the one that compounds, because a better sentence helps once and a better vault helps forever.

04Static Context and Dynamic Context

Context splits cleanly into two kinds, and confusing them is what makes people either paste too much or trust stale facts. Your vault holds the first kind. Weeks 3 and 4 add the second.

S

Static context: the things that are true this quarter

Your brand voice, your personas, your positioning, your product facts, your banned words, your campaign lessons. It changes on the timescale of strategy, not hours. You write it once and maintain it deliberately.

Lives in: your vault, loaded up front. This is today's build. It is the part that compounds.

D

Dynamic context: the things that are true right now

Today's campaign numbers, this morning's competitor announcement, the lead who filled the form ten minutes ago, live inventory. It is stale the moment you write it down, so you never write it down. You fetch it.

Lives in: tools and live connections, fetched at the moment of the request. Week 3 builds this with a Claude Code harness.

The mistake to avoid all quarter

Never put dynamic facts in your static vault. The instant you type "Q3 spend to date: N2.4m" into a note, you have created a fact that will be wrong next week and will keep being confidently repeated by your AI. Static notes hold where to look and what it means. Numbers that move get fetched, or they get a visible date and an owner.

The tradeoff nobody tells you about Gemini Notebook

Its retrieval is genuinely the best of the five and the citations are excellent, which makes it superb for interrogating documents you did not write. But Google's own help documents the boundaries: sources are capped per notebook, there are daily query limits, and notebooks cannot be duplicated. Most decisively for this course, it is a web app. Week 3's automation cannot open a notebook the way it opens a file.

Verdict: excellent research companion, wrong shape for a vault your automations must read. Use it alongside, not instead.

The tradeoff nobody tells you about Notion

Best-in-class for teams, permissions and shared editing, and Claude has an official connector that can search a connected workspace. But your content lives in Notion's structure, exports are lossy for some block types, and every automation goes through the network and an API key rather than reading a local file.

Verdict: the right answer if your marketing team already runs on Notion. Heavier than you need for a one-person vault built today.

The tradeoff nobody tells you about Logseq

Technically strong and genuinely private. The catch is timing: Logseq's own April 2026 announcement confirms the project has split into two products, with the file-based Markdown version continuing as Logseq OG, receiving security and framework updates but no new features, while the database version becomes the main line. That is a fine choice for an existing user and an awkward one to start a paid course on this month.

Verdict: sound tool, unsettled moment. Not what I will put your client work on today.

The tradeoff nobody tells you about a plain folder

It genuinely works, and it is exactly the same storage format Obsidian uses. What you give up is the connective tissue: clickable links between notes, tag search, a graph view showing gaps, and the properties panel that makes structured fields easy to keep consistent. For a non-technical user maintaining 40 notes, that tissue is the difference between a living vault and a folder nobody opens.

Verdict: your fallback if Obsidian will not install. You lose convenience, not portability.

The recommendation, and the evidence for it

Obsidian stays the primary tool for this course. Not from habit. Because of one decisive property that the others cannot match: Obsidian's own documentation states that notes are stored as Markdown-formatted plain text files in a folder on your local file system, and that because they are plain text you can edit and manage them with other text editors and file managers. That is exactly the shape the rest of this course needs. In Week 3, a Claude Code harness reads those files. In Week 4, the same harness gains memory and more skills. A web app cannot do that, and an API-only workspace adds a network hop you do not need for a local vault.

Structure comes free too: Obsidian's properties are stored in a YAML frontmatter block at the top of each note, so type, status and last-updated are queryable fields rather than prose. Its Bases core plugin builds database-style table views, and its docs confirm all that data still lives in your local Markdown files and their properties. You get database behaviour without a database.

What would change my mind: if your team already runs on Notion, build it there and accept the API path in Week 3. If you never intend to automate and only want to interrogate documents conversationally, Gemini Notebook beats Obsidian at that specific job. Neither is true for you today, so we build in Obsidian.

One honest limitation of the pick

Obsidian has no AI retrieval of its own. The vault does not answer questions; it is the library your model reads. That is a real gap compared to Gemini Notebook, and it is why Section 09 wires the vault into Claude rather than stopping at the files. Know what you are choosing: maximum portability and automation-readiness, in exchange for doing the retrieval wiring yourself.

06How a Knowledge Base Is Built: Seven Ideas

Before you touch the keyboard, get the design. These seven ideas are the whole architecture of a vault, and none of them is technical. Read them in order, because each one builds on the last.

1

Markdown: formatting that survives everything

Markdown is plain text with a few punctuation conventions. # Heading makes a heading. **bold** makes bold. - item makes a bullet. That is most of it. Because the file stays readable text, a human, a model, and an automation can all read the same file without conversion.

Why it matters to you: a Word file is a container you need software to open. A Markdown file is the content itself.

2

Folders: one shelf per kind of thing

Folders group notes by type, not by campaign or by date. Voice in one folder, personas in another, product facts in another. Type-based folders stay stable for years; campaign-based folders rot within two quarters.

The test: if a new note has an obvious home without you thinking, your folders are right.

3

Links: the connective tissue

Typing [[Persona - Operations Lead]] inside any note creates a clickable link to that note. Obsidian's documentation calls these internal links and notes that linking builds a network of knowledge. It also updates links automatically when you rename a file, which is what stops a vault from decaying.

Marketing payoff: your campaign retro links to the persona it targeted, which links to the voice guide it used. One click each way.

4

Tags: cross-cutting labels

A tag is a keyword starting with a hash, such as #persona, or a list under a tags property. Folders answer "what kind of note is this", tags answer "what else does it touch": a channel, a quarter, a status.

Rule of thumb: one folder per note, several tags per note. If you are agonising over which folder, that is a tag.

5

Properties and YAML frontmatter: the fields that make retrieval possible

At the very top of a note, between two lines of three dashes, you can list fields: type, status, updated, owner. Obsidian's docs confirm properties are stored in this YAML frontmatter block and hold structured data such as text, links, dates, checkboxes and numbers.

This is what makes "load only active personas" possible instead of "load everything and hope". It is the single highest-value habit in the vault.

6

The Index, also called a Map of Content

One note that links to everything and says in one line what each thing is for. It is the front door for a human and the table of contents for a model. When you point an AI at the vault, this is the note it should read first.

Without an index, a vault is a pile. With one, it is a library.

7

Source of truth: one fact, one home

Every fact lives in exactly one note. Everything else links to it. The moment your price appears in three notes, you have three prices, and two of them will be wrong within a quarter.

This is the discipline that keeps a vault trustworthy at 200 notes. Duplication is the enemy, not disorganisation.

Naming: boring, consistent, and searchable

File names are context too. Anthropic's engineering write-up makes this point about agents reading a file system: folder hierarchies, naming conventions and timestamps all provide signals that help both humans and agents understand how and when to use information. A note called notes2.md tells a model nothing. A note called persona-operations-lead.md tells it who the note is about before it opens it.

RuleDo thisNot this
Lead with the typepersona-operations-lead.mdBola notes.md
Lowercase, hyphens, no spacesbrand-voice-guide.mdBrand Voice Guide FINAL v3.md
No version numbers in namesproduct-facts.mdproduct-facts-v2-updated.md

Why no version numbers: the note is the current truth. If you need history, the date field in the frontmatter carries it. Two files named v2 and v3 is two sources of truth, which is the problem from idea 7.

Retrieval, in plain terms: how the right slice gets picked

You have 40 notes and a request about one email. Three ways the right ones get loaded, from crudest to best:

Paste everything→ Wastes the window, invites context rot
You choose the files→ Accurate, but you do it every single time
Index plus properties→ The model reads the index, sees types and dates→ Opens only what it needs

The third pattern is what your structure is for. Anthropic describes the same idea as keeping lightweight identifiers such as file paths and loading data into context just in time, rather than pre-processing everything up front. Your index is the list of identifiers. Your properties are the filters.

07Pitfalls, Before You Hit Them

Every one of these has cost somebody a week. You are about to start building, so meet them now rather than in October.

⚠ The vault that is really a diary

Notes written as narrative for yourself instead of facts for a reader. "We had a good chat about tone and decided to loosen it up a bit" is a diary entry. "Tone: contractions allowed, sentences under 20 words" is context.

⚠ Fifty notes, zero used

People build the whole structure and never wire it to the AI, so nothing changes. Six real notes wired in beats fifty notes nobody reads. We wire in at Section 09, deliberately early.

⚠ Dynamic facts pasted into static notes

Today's spend, this week's follower count, the current lead total. They are wrong by Friday and your AI will repeat them with total confidence. Numbers that move need a date and an owner, or they need fetching.

⚠ The same fact in four places

You update the price in the product note and forget the pricing note, the FAQ note, and the persona note. Now the model picks one at random. One fact, one home, everything else links.

⚠ Dumping the whole vault into every request

It feels thorough. It is the documented path to context rot, it costs more tokens, and it makes answers vaguer. Load the slice the task needs.

⚠ Confidential material in a general tool

Unreleased pricing, customer names, contracts, salary data, anything under NDA. Once it is uploaded you cannot unsee it. Section 12 gives you the rule set. Read it before you upload anything real.

⚠ No date on anything

Six months on you cannot tell which notes are current. Nobody trusts the vault, so everybody works around it, and it dies. Every note gets an updated field, no exceptions.

⚠ Perfection before use

Waiting until the voice guide is complete before pointing the AI at it. It will never be complete. It gets good by being corrected, and it can only be corrected once it is in use.

08Build It: Exact Setup Steps

Hands on keyboard from here. Follow along. If you fall behind, say so and we stop, because everything after this point uses what you build now.

Part A: Install Obsidian and create the vault

  1. Go to obsidian.md and click Download. Pick your platform: Windows, Mac, or Linux. Obsidian's pricing page states it is free to use with no sign-up required, so there is no account step and no card.
  2. Open the installer and complete it as normal. Launch Obsidian.
  3. On the welcome screen choose Create new vault.
  4. Name it brand-context-vault.
  5. For location, choose a folder you will find again and that your backup covers. Your Documents folder is fine. Write the full path on paper now. You will need it in Week 3 when the harness reads these files.
  6. Click Create. You now have an empty vault, which on disk is simply a folder holding text files.

Sanity check: open your normal file manager, navigate to that folder, and confirm you can see it. That folder is your vault. Obsidian is just a comfortable window onto it.

Part B: Create the six folders

In Obsidian's left sidebar, use the New folder icon. Create these six, named exactly like this:

brand-context-vault/ ├── index.md ← the front door, we write this next ├── 01-voice/ ← how we sound ├── 02-personas/ ← who we talk to ├── 03-positioning/ ← competitors and our differentiators ├── 04-product/ ← what we sell, facts only ├── 05-campaigns/ ← what we learned, one retro per campaign └── 06-guardrails/ ← what we never say

Six folders, numbered so they stay in this order on screen. The numbering is for your eyes, and it also tells a model the intended reading order.

Note what is missing: no folder called "misc", "temp", or "2026". Those are where vaults go to die. If a note has no home here, that is a signal the note is not context.

01-voice/brand-voice-guide.md
---
title: Brand Voice Guide
type: voice
status: active
updated: 2026-08-13
owner: [your name]
tags: [voice, foundation]
---

# Brand Voice Guide

## In one sentence
We sound like [an experienced operations manager explaining something useful to
a peer]: direct, concrete, never talking down.

## Voice rules, in order of importance
1. Lead with the outcome, not the feature.
2. Sentences under 20 words. Break the rule only for rhythm.
3. Active voice. Name who does the thing.
4. Concrete numbers over adjectives. "Cuts dispatch time by 40 minutes",
   not "dramatically faster".
5. Second person. The reader is "you". We are "we".
6. Contractions are fine. We are not writing a legal notice.

## Words we use
| Use this | Not this | Why |
|---|---|---|
| customers | users | We sell to businesses with names, not accounts |
| dispatch | logistics operations | Our buyers say dispatch |
| costs you | represents an investment | Plain money language |
| we recommend | best practice dictates | We advise, the client decides |

## Do say
- "Here is what we found"
- "This will not work if [honest condition]"
- "Our customers tell us"
- Specific numbers with the period they cover

## Never say
- See [[do-not-says]] for the full banned list. It wins over anything here.

## Tone by situation
| Situation | Target | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| LinkedIn post | Direct, one clear idea, opinionated | Thought-leader padding |
| Sales email | Useful in the first line, short | Warm-up paragraphs |
| Landing page | Outcome first, proof second | Feature lists |
| Support reply | Plain, accountable, no hedging | Corporate apology voice |
| Investor update | Precise, numbers first | Optimism without figures |

## Written examples, real and approved
Good, and why: "Your drivers lose 40 minutes a day to manual check-ins.
This removes that." Outcome first, concrete number, second person, 12 words.

Bad, and why: "We are excited to announce our innovative solution that
revolutionises fleet management." Announcement about us, three banned words,
zero information.

## Sound like, and not like
- Closer to: [a named publication or brand your reader respects]
- Not: [a named brand that is too formal], [a named brand that is too casual]

12The Correction Loop: Every Fix Becomes Permanent

This is the habit that separates a vault that compounds from a folder that rots. It takes thirty seconds per correction and it is the highest-return thirty seconds in your week.

The rule

When you correct an AI's output, you have two choices. Fix the draft, and fix it again next month. Or fix the note, and never fix it again. A correction you only make in the chat is a correction you will make forever.

1

Notice the correction you are about to make

You are mid-edit, deleting a word or fixing a claim. Stop for a second. That edit is information the vault does not have yet.

2

Name the note that should have prevented it

Wrong word: do-not-says. Wrong audience assumption: the persona. Wrong claim: product-facts. Wrong tone for the channel: channel-tone-rules. There is almost always exactly one right home.

3

Write one line into that note, today

Not a rewrite. One line. "Never say seamless" takes four seconds and pays out for years. Update the updated date while you are there.

4

Re-upload the changed note to the project

Otherwise the model keeps reading the old version and you will conclude the loop does not work. Edit in the vault, push to the project.

Worked examples from real sessions

The correction you madeThe weak instinctThe vault fix
Deleted "seamless integration" Add "avoid buzzwords" to the next prompt Add seamless to the banned table in do-not-says
Rewrote the opening because it flattered the reader Say "be more direct" next time Add a voice rule: "no warm-up. First line carries information"
Fixed a price the model invented Correct it in the chat and move on Put the real number in product-facts. This is a source-of-truth gap, and it is urgent
Changed the angle because finance cares about cost, not time Mention it in every finance prompt from now on Add the cost framing to persona-finance-lead
Cut a claim you cannot actually prove Remember not to use it Add to the Not Yet True list in product-facts, with the reason

Pattern to notice: the weak instinct always puts the fix in a prompt, which is disposable. The vault fix puts it somewhere permanent. Same effort, completely different half-life.

13Hygiene, Privacy and Permissions

A vault is a liability as well as an asset. Two things will hurt you: facts that quietly go stale, and material that should never have been uploaded. Both are preventable with rules you set today.

Context hygiene: keeping it true

Every note carries a date and an owner

The updated and owner fields are not decoration. They are how you and your model both judge whether a fact can still be trusted. A note with no date is a rumour.

Monthly review, fifteen minutes

Sort by updated, oldest first. Anything over six months: confirm, correct, or mark status: archive. Do not delete history, retire it, so nobody resurrects a withdrawn claim.

Handling conflicts on purpose

When two notes disagree, the librarian instruction makes the model say so rather than choose. Then you fix it properly: decide which note owns the fact, delete the duplicate, and link to the owner instead.

Archive rather than delete

Set status: archive and leave the note in place with the reason it was retired. This is exactly what the withdrawn claims table is for. Deleted context comes back as somebody's "good idea" next quarter.

Security and privacy, the rules that are not negotiable

Never put in a vault you upload: customer names or contact details, contracts, unreleased pricing you are not ready to leak, salary or HR data, anything under NDA, API keys or passwords, or personal data belonging to identifiable people.

What to do instead: your vault holds patterns, not records. "Operations leads at 40 to 200 vehicle businesses object to training time" is context. "Bola Adeyemi at [named company] said this on 3 June" is a customer record, and it belongs in your CRM under whatever consent you actually have. Anonymise the quote, keep the insight.

Permissions: who can read what

Three separate questions people collapse into one, which is how leaks happen.

QuestionWhat actually governs itYour move today
Who can read the files? Your device and whatever syncs it. Obsidian's docs state notes are stored locally on your file system, and its pricing page states your data is stored locally on your device and inaccessible to them, with Sync secured by end-to-end encryption if you enable it. Decide now whether this folder is backed up or synced, and to where. Write it down.
Who can read the uploaded copy? Your AI account and its plan terms. On Claude, project sharing with colleagues is a Team and Enterprise capability. Check your own plan's terms before uploading anything sensitive. Do not assume.
What can a connected AI reach? Its permissions in the source system. Anthropic's connector documentation is explicit: Claude inherits each person's permissions, so if someone cannot access a file, channel or record in the source system, the connector cannot reach it from Claude either. Grant the narrowest access that works. Review it when people change roles.

Practical version for a one-person marketing function: keep one vault with no confidential records in it, and never upload anything you would not email to a colleague outside your department.
